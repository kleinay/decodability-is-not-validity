\begin{abstract}
Two concept probes can be equally accurate at predicting a concept from a language model's hidden states while their scores contain different mixtures of the target concept and a correlated nuisance attribute. In four target--nuisance settings (two aspect sentiments in CEBaB; occupation and gender in BiasBios; Amazon review sentiment with negation, and with first-person framing), two LLMs, and a population of concept extractors, we compare decodability with discriminant evidence: the score's dependence on the nuisance at fixed target, and its response to input edits intended to change only the nuisance, filtered by two LLM judges and compared against placebo edits. Under correlated training data, extractors that are statistically equivalent in held-out AUROC differ up to several-fold in nuisance sensitivity in three of the four settings, and decodability is not a reliable proxy for it: their rank correlation changes sign across settings. The effect follows the probe's training data, not the nuisance's natural association with the target: first-person framing, unrelated to sentiment in natural reviews, is absorbed at least as much as negation, which co-occurs with negative sentiment, and decorrelated training removes the systematic effect. Selecting extractors with nuisance-aware development diagnostics, which need nuisance labels but no counterfactuals, lowers counterfactual nuisance sensitivity wherever the default choice is sensitive, at a small accuracy cost. Concept measurements should be validated, not just decoded.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Probes, concept activation vectors and subspace readouts turn a hidden state into a scalar proxy for a concept $T$ in the input \citep{alain2017understanding,kim2018tcav,belinkov2022probing,zou2023representation,marks2024geometry}; the usual evidence for that reading is decodability, i.e.\ predicting held-out labels of $T$. Decodability cannot identify \emph{what} the score measures. Call a \emph{nuisance} $N$ an attribute that is distinct from $T$ but may be correlated with it (e.g., food sentiment when $T$ is service sentiment). Take binary $T$ and $N$ and two scores, $s_1=T$ and $s_2=T+\alpha N$ with $0<\alpha<1$. Every input with $T{=}1$ scores at least 1 under $s_2$ and every input with $T{=}0$ at most $\alpha<1$, so $s_2$, like $s_1$, ranks all positives above all negatives: both have AUROC 1. Yet only $s_2$ changes when $N$ changes and $T$ does not.

In measurement theory this is a question of construct validity, an evidential, multidimensional property of a score's interpretation \citep{cronbach1955construct,messick1995validity,jacobs2021measurement,bean2025measuring}. Part of that evidence is \emph{discriminant} \citep{campbell1959convergent}: measures of correlated constructs may correlate, but the score should not depend on the other construct \emph{once the target is held fixed}.

That accuracy cannot detect such dependence is not new: probes trained on spuriously correlated data can rely on the spurious attribute \citep{kumar2022probing}, probing accuracy need not reflect use by the model \citep{hewitt2019designing,ravichander2021probing,elazar2021amnesic,tiwari2026decodability,sudheendra2026decodability}, and interventions on representations trade completeness against selectivity \citep{canby2025reliable,huang2024ravel}. Recent work tests whether probes and SAEs disentangle known concepts \citep{mueller2026isolation}, compares concept-subspace estimators \citep{naowarat2026framework}, and shows that accurate probes can be misaligned \citep{lysnaeslarsen2025probing}, that probe readings depend on design choices \citep{noel2026probe} and correlated features \citep{shen2026beyond}, and that embedding-based measures need counterfactual validation \citep{li2026proxy}. We ask a narrower question about scalar readouts: among accuracy-matched extractors, how much does sensitivity to a named nuisance under input edits vary, does it persist without training-set correlation, and can development diagnostics choose better? We answer with a population study over four settings and two models, using judge-filtered, placebo-controlled edits, including two nuisances edited on the same reviews (\S\ref{sec:results}), and compare selection criteria (\S\ref{sec:selection}). Our interventions edit the \emph{input text} of frozen models: unlike work asking whether a decodable direction drives behavior \citep{tiwari2026decodability,sudheendra2026decodability}, we ask what a score tracks in the input.

\section{Measuring nuisance selectivity}
\label{sec:battery}

An \emph{extractor} maps a hidden state $h$ to $s(h)=w^\top z(h)+b$, with $z$ the standardization on training data; subspace methods have a linear readout too, so every extractor is a scalar score. Each setting supplies a binary target $T$, a binary nuisance $N$, and edits of the input. Let \mbox{$\rho=P(N{=}1\mid T{=}1)$} \mbox{${}=P(N{=}0\mid T{=}0)$} with balanced $T$. Extractors are trained at $\rhot\in\{0.5,0.7,0.9\}$; test metrics reweight the four $(T,N)$ cells of one fixed test pool.

\noindent\textbf{\dec{}}: AUROC of $s$ for $T$ at $\rho=\rhot$ (in-distribution decodability, the standard criterion).\\
\textbf{$|$\leak{}$|$}: $\tfrac12\sum_{t}|\mathrm{AUROC}(s\to N\mid T{=}t)-0.5|$, dependence on $N$ with the target held fixed.\\
\textbf{\conf{}}: AUROC for $T$ on the cells with $T\neq N$ (a fully reversed correlation). A stress test, not a validity measure: there, $s=-N$ also ranks $T$ perfectly.\\
\textbf{$\cfn$}: mean change in $s$ under edits that flip only $N$, signed toward the $N$ value that co-occurs with $T{=}1$ in training, divided by the mean score gap between the $T$ classes on the decorrelated pool ($\Delta_T$). As a signed mean can hide opposite changes, we also report $|\cfn|$ (mean absolute change over $\Delta_T$) and compare with \emph{placebo} edits that change neither variable.\\
\textbf{$\cft$}: the same quantity for edits that flip $T$ (CEBaB only): convergent evidence.

\paragraph{Data and edits} (Table~\ref{tab:data}). \textbf{CEBaB} \citep{abraham2022cebab}: restaurant reviews with aspect labels and human-written counterfactual edits; we pair each original test review with edits that flip food (or, for $\cft$, service) sentiment and leave the other aspect's label unchanged; splits are disjoint by review family. \textbf{BiasBios} \citep{dearteaga2019bias}: surgeon vs.\ nurse biographies; $N$ is the subject's gender, edited in both directions by an LLM (gpt-oss-120b) that swaps the subject's pronouns, gendered nouns and first name and nothing else (automatically checked). \textbf{Amazon} \citep{mcauley2013hidden,zhang2015character}: short product reviews (8--40 words), $T$ = negative sentiment, with two nuisances defined by word lists, so whether an edit flips them is checked exactly: \emph{negation} (\emph{not}, \emph{n't}, \emph{never}, \emph{no}, \dots; in training data present in \amzNegNeg{} of negative and \amzNegPos{} of positive reviews) and \emph{first person} (the writer refers to themselves: \emph{I}, \emph{my}, \emph{we}, \dots; \amzFpNeg{} vs.\ \amzFpPos{}, i.e.\ uncorrelated). Each is its own setting (Amazon-Neg, Amazon-1P). gpt-oss-120b proposes phrase replacements that add or remove the nuisance while keeping the meaning and the strength of the opinion (``doesn't work''$\to$``fails''; ``Would recommend''$\to$``I would recommend''), applied by exact string match (Appendix~\ref{app:edits}). We keep up to 1{,}100/250/400 training/development/test texts per $(T,N)$ cell and train on samples of 2{,}000.

\paragraph{Edit validation.} Two judge models, neither identical to a probed model (Qwen3-32B, run locally, and gpt-oss-120b; for Amazon, Qwen3.8-27B and gpt-oss-120b via an API; the Qwen judges share a model family with Qwen2.5-7B), see each pair in random order, without being told which text is edited or which attribute should change, and say whether the target and the nuisance are the same, rate each text's fluency, and flag other meaning changes. Checked against human labels and planted errors (Appendix~\ref{app:judgeval}), a nuisance edit is \emph{accepted} if both judges find the target unchanged and the nuisance changed, with no major other meaning change and no fluency drop. For Amazon the nuisance flip is checked by the word list instead (the judges missed real flips in a pilot), and both judges must also find the strength of the opinion unchanged. All counterfactual results use accepted pairs only (Appendix~\ref{app:edits}). Placebo edits replace neutral words with synonyms.

\paragraph{Models and extractors.} Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25} and Gemma-2-9B-it \citep{gemma2024gemma2}, frozen; residual stream mean-pooled over non-special tokens at 7 depths. Per layer: difference of class means; $\ell_2$ logistic regression ($C\in\{10^{-3},\dots,10\}$); partial least squares ($k\in\{2,4,8,16\}$; $k{=}1$ equals the mean difference and is dropped); and a \emph{nuisance-erased} probe that projects out the within-class nuisance mean differences, estimated on the training sample only (cf.\ \citealp{ravfogel2020null,belrose2023leace}) before logistic regression. The first three families use no nuisance labels (\emph{standard}, 70 per setting); the erased probe adds 35. Each is fit on three training samples. An extractor is \emph{near-best} if its test \dec{} is within 0.01 of the best, and \emph{equivalent} to the best if a paired-bootstrap 90\% CI of the \dec{} difference lies inside $\pm0.01$ (TOST at $\alpha=0.05$). Because the reference is selected on the test pool, these equivalence results are descriptive; the selection comparisons (\S\ref{sec:selection}) choose on development data only.

\paragraph{Accuracy-matched extractors differ in nuisance sensitivity.} With correlated training data ($\rhot=0.9$), extractors equivalent to the best in decodability span $\cfn$ from \eqCFmincebabQNine{} to \eqCFmaxcebabQNine{} on CEBaB (Qwen; Gemma \eqCFmincebabGNine--\eqCFmaxcebabGNine): for some, rewriting only the food sentence of a review shifts the ``service'' score most of the way to the other service class. On BiasBios \dec{} is at ceiling for nearly all extractors (\eqNbiosQNine{} equivalent to the best), while gender edits shift the occupation score by \eqCFminbiosQNine--\eqCFmaxbiosQNine{} of the surgeon--nurse gap; on Amazon-1P, adding or removing a first-person reference shifts the sentiment score of equivalent extractors by \eqCFminamzfpQNine--\eqCFmaxamzfpQNine{} (Gemma \eqCFminamzfpGNine--\eqCFmaxamzfpGNine). On Amazon-Neg, every near-best extractor is only weakly sensitive (\nbCFminamznegQNine--\nbCFmaxamznegQNine). Rankings by $\cfn$ agree on all vs.\ accepted edits ($\geq$\robAllValMin), under SD normalization ($\geq$\robSDMin) and, at $\rhot=0.9$, between edit directions ($\geq$\robDirMinNine; Appendix~\ref{app:edits}); placebo edits move scores little (median $|\cfn|$ \placeboMin--\placeboMax).

\paragraph{Same reviews, two nuisances.} First person is unrelated to sentiment in natural reviews, yet under the same training correlation ($\rhot=0.9$) the median $\cfn$ of competitive extractors is \sgnamzfpNine{} for first person and \sgnamznegNine{} for negation (an increase over decorrelated training of \incamzfp{} vs.\ \incamzneg): the probe's training data, not the nuisance's natural association, produces the reliance. Negation edits move scores by a similar absolute amount at any training correlation (median $|\cfn|$ \absamznegFive{} at $\rhot=0.5$, \absamznegNine{} at 0.9; placebo \plcamznegNine), and at $\rhot=0.5$ against the nuisance direction, mostly when a negation is removed (\dirNegamznegFive): consistent with ``not good''$\to$``bad'' sharpening the opinion, which the strength check does not fully exclude, rather than with reliance on the nuisance.

\paragraph{With decorrelated training, the directional effect mostly disappears.} At $\rhot=0.5$, near-best extractors show little net nuisance shift on BiasBios and both Amazon settings (signed $\cfn$ within $\pm0.08$) and less on CEBaB (\nbCFmincebabQFive--\nbCFmaxcebabQFive, Qwen), where example-level shifts remain (median $|\cfn|$ \absCFcebabQFive{} vs.\ signed \sgnCFcebabQFive; placebo \placebocebabQFive, for smaller edits). Training-set correlation drives the systematic reliance on the nuisance. A second source is geometric: on CEBaB the target and within-class food directions are aligned (cosine \cosMincebab--\cosMaxcebab), and this observational geometry approximately predicts the food sensitivity of the difference of means trained on decorrelated data (mean absolute error \geomMAEcebab; synthetic illustration in Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic}).

\paragraph{Decodability is not a reliable proxy.} Among competitive standard extractors at $\rhot=0.9$, the rank correlation between \dec{} and $\cfn$ ranges from \corrCFmin{} to \corrCFmax{} and changes sign across settings (descriptive intervals: Appendix~\ref{app:more}). Target edits do not arbitrate either: on CEBaB every near-best extractor responds fully to \emph{service} edits ($\cft$ \cfTmin--\cfTmax) while its food sensitivity ranges over \cfNnbMin--\cfNnbMax.

\paragraph{A fixed-method comparison.} With layer and hyperparameter chosen on development data, the logistic and the nuisance-erased probe are equivalent in \dec{} at $\rhot=0.9$ on BiasBios (90\% CI [\fpLobiosQNine, \fpHibiosQNine], Qwen) and Amazon-1P, yet $\cfn$ is \fpCFlrbiosQNine{} vs.\ \fpCFnebiosQNine{} and \fpCFlramzfpQNine{} vs.\ \fpCFneamzfpQNine{} (Appendix~\ref{app:more}). Erasure lowers but does not remove nuisance sensitivity.

\section{Discussion}

Our claim is narrower than ``probes are invalid'': decodability is not a reliable proxy for selectivity with respect to a named nuisance, and probes rely on a nuisance whenever their training data correlate it with the target. A claim that a direction \emph{measures} a concept should name plausible nuisances and report discriminant diagnostics and, where possible, nuisance-only edits for each; passing them is necessary evidence, not construct validity \citep{messick1995validity,licht2025measuring}.

\section*{Limitations}

Each setting examines one named nuisance (Amazon: two, separately); selectivity for unexamined nuisances is unknown. Edits are filtered by two LLM judges, not by new human annotation. We validate the judges against CEBaB's human labels and with planted errors, and recompute the results under every filter (Appendix~\ref{app:judgeval}), but human labels exist only for CEBaB, the judges agree only poorly to moderately with each other, and accepted pairs may be a selective subset; the code generates a blinded stratified sample for human annotation. LLM edits can change more than the nuisance: two LLM readers rate \rdWrongNeg{} of accepted negation edits (Amazon-Neg) as changing the opinion, its intensity or a fact, or as broken, mostly where a negation was added; negation edits can shift the intensity of an opinion despite the strength check. $\cfn$ is normalized by the class score gap, which is unstable for weak extractors, so we analyze competitive ones; an effect-size normalization (by the within-cell score SD, akin to Cohen's $d$) ranks extractors similarly ($\geq$\robSDMin). Correlated regimes are produced by resampling; they match natural rates for BiasBios ($P(\text{male}\mid\text{surgeon})=0.85$, $P(\text{female}\mid\text{nurse})=0.91$) and approximately CEBaB (0.80, 0.73) and Amazon-Neg, and are a stress test for Amazon-1P. Strict equivalence is established for few extractors on CEBaB. We study linear readouts of mean-pooled (and, as an ablation, last-token) representations of two 7--9B models; nonlinear probes, SAE features and other models are untested. Our measures concern what a score tracks in text, not what the model computes with it.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

BiasBios involves gender, and the appendix setting (Civil) religious and sexual identity. Concept scores used for auditing or monitoring can encode such attributes while passing accuracy checks; our diagnostics complement, and do not replace, fairness evaluation. The gender swap follows the dataset's binary annotation.
