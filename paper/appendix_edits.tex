\paragraph{Amazon edits.} For each test review, gpt-oss-120b receives an instruction to add (or remove every instance of) the nuisance with an exact paraphrase that keeps every fact and the strength of the opinion, and returns JSON phrase replacements, which we apply by exact string match, so only the named phrases change; it may decline. Automatic checks require that the nuisance flips according to the word list, that at most 35\% of the words change, and that the length is stable. Examples of accepted edits: negation added, ``spaces between word were missing''$\to$``\dots{} not present''; removed, ``poorly written and not well thought out''$\to$``\dots{} ill-conceived'', ``I had not received the driver cd''$\to$``I had failed to receive \dots''; first person added, ``Received this book promptly''$\to$``I received \dots''; removed, ``is it just me?''$\to$``is this the only case?''. Prompts were developed on 100 test reviews per nuisance and frozen before a held-out check and the full run. The model declines most often when asked to add first person to a review without a natural place for it, so Amazon-1P has fewer additions than removals; every direction-specific result is reported separately (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). Because negation is part of how sentiment is expressed, a paraphrase that removes it can still sharpen an opinion; both judges answer an explicit question about the strength of the opinion, and the residual effect at $\rhot=0.5$ is discussed in \S\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Validating the judges without new annotation}\label{app:judgeval}
\paragraph{Human labels (CEBaB).} CEBaB's edits were written by crowd workers, and five other workers labelled the food and service sentiment (positive, negative, not mentioned) of every original and edited review. We judged \jvN{} (original, food or service edit) pairs from the test and development families with both judges, including all \jvNbad{} edits whose intended aspect label did \emph{not} change according to the human majority (\jvBadRate{} of the set, oversampled). On whether each aspect is the same in the two texts, gpt-oss-120b agrees with the human majority in \jvGptQ{} of pairs, as often as a single held-out worker agrees with the majority of the other four (\jvHumanQ); Qwen3-32B agrees in \jvQwenQ, mostly because it reports changes the workers did not. Applying the acceptance rule, both judges reject \jvRejBad{} of the failed edits, and \jvPrec{} of accepted pairs are correct by the human labels (vs.\ \jvGoodRate{} of all judged pairs); the price is recall: half of the edits correct by the human labels are rejected (\jvRecall{} kept), in part for fluency or other meaning changes that the human labels do not cover.

\paragraph{Planted errors.} On edits both judges had accepted (50 per type and setting, disjoint sources), we planted one known error after the nuisance edit, keeping the word-list nuisance status: gpt-oss-120b proposed a phrase replacement that reverses the opinion, makes it clearly stronger or weaker, changes the occupation, or changes a minor fact; by rule, we garbled the text (swap, duplicate and drop a word) or reverted one gendered substitution (mixed gender cues). Table~\ref{tab:planted} gives how often the judges still accept. Reversed opinions, changed occupations and garbled text pass both judges in at most \plBadMax{} of cases; in the passing reversals the planted phrase mostly changes one aspect of a mixed review, not the overall opinion, and garbled reviews pass when the judges already rated the source non-fluent, which the rule allows. gpt-oss-120b alone accepts \plStrGptMin--\plStrGptMax{} of strength changes, so the strength check rests mainly on the Qwen judge (both: $\leq$\plStrBothMax). Mixed gender cues pass both in \plMixBoth. Minor fact changes pass often (\plFactMin--\plFactMax): the rule tolerates minor meaning changes by design, and some planted changes are trivial. Re-judging unmodified accepted edits in a new presentation order keeps \plCleanMin--\plCleanMax{} of them, a measure of judge noise.

\paragraph{Reading accepted Amazon edits.} Two further LLM readers not involved in editing or judging (Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1) each labelled the same 100 accepted Amazon edits (25 per setting and direction, judge verdicts hidden) as ok, unnatural (meaning kept, stilted wording), or wrong (opinion, strength or a fact changed, or broken text); they agree on \rdAgree{} of items ($\kappa=\rdKappa$). They rate \rdWrongFp{} of Amazon-1P edits wrong and \rdWrongNeg{} of Amazon-Neg edits, \rdWrongNegAdd{} of negation additions, which mostly replace a word by a weaker negated antonym (``one-of-a-kind''$\to$``not ordinary''); a further share is unnatural but meaning-preserving (any non-ok label: Amazon-1P \rdNotOkFp, Amazon-Neg \rdNotOkNeg). Removing first person from a superlative can broaden it (``the worst movie I have ever seen''$\to$``the worst movie ever seen''). Neither reader is a human, so this is a further check of edit quality, not a human validation.
